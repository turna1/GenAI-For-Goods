Perform dynamic measurements during train operation through techniques such as wheel impact loads (WIL), car-body vibration tests, or bogie dynamics analysis to gauge the overall structural health of the rail system. This will enable you to address problems before they escalate into major failures. 
4. Conduct periodic fatigue assessments on critical components of the rail infrastructure such as the welded joints between the rails and turnouts, as well as the fishplates holding them together, which should be replaced when necessary. 
5. Regularly test the rail bond quality at various locations along the line since poor adhesion between the rail head and base material leads to rapid wear, reducing service life.
 6. Use ground penetrating radar (GPR) technology to scan the railbed for anomalies concealed beneath the surface, especially where older rails have been installed over long periods without significant replacements. 
7. Ensure proper cleaning methods are employed to remove dirt, grit, and other pollutants from the railheads regularly; otherwise, these particles build upon each other, leading to higher rolling resistance, reduced efficiency, and increased wear rates.
 8. Implement routine maintenance routines, such as tightening fastenings, replacing worn-out elements, renewing worn-out components, removing contaminants, repacking bearings, monitoring the entire rail system's performance, and documenting all activities and findings. 
9. Establish precise schedules and timeframes for performing maintenance tasks, depending on the specific context and nature of the rail environment. For instance, high traffic lines warrant more frequent inspections than less busy ones.
